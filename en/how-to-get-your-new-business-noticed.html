Customers & Visibility

How to Get Your New Business Noticed (Even With No Marketing Budget)

By Sarah Mitchell · Updated September 29, 2026 · 10 min read

The sign is up, the EIN came through, and the phone is quiet. That's normal: nobody can pick a business they can't see. Here's what to do, week by week, during your first month so people find you on Google, on Apple Maps and in AI assistants, and so you can go out and land your first customers yourself. Most of it costs nothing but time.

The short answer

To get your new business noticed, first show up where people already search: a verified Google Business Profile, the same contact details on a handful of solid directories, and a clear page that says what you do. Only then go looking for customers: neighbors, complementary businesses, and companies you contact directly.

The order matters. A prospect you email will almost always google your name before replying. If nothing comes up, your message goes straight to the trash.

Week 1: how to get my business on Google Maps for free

Your Google Business Profile is free, and it's the first thing to set up. Google says it plainly: you can add or claim your business "at no charge." If someone calls offering to "put you on Google" for a fee, that's not the profile itself they're selling.

This is the box that shows up when someone types "plumber near me" or "coffee shop open now": the map pin, your hours, your reviews and a button to call you.

The steps, in order

  • Search your business name on Google first. A profile sometimes already exists, built from public data. If so, claim it instead of creating a duplicate.
  • Create the profile at business.google.com/add, using your real business name with no extra keywords stuffed in.
  • Pick the most specific category. "Bakery" beats "Food store." The category is a big part of which searches you show up in.
  • Verify it. Depending on your business, Google offers a phone call or text, an email, a live video call, or a postcard with a code.

Plan for some lag. Google says verification reviews take up to 5 business days, and most postcard codes arrive within 14 days. Start on opening day, not three weeks in.

How to get my business noticed on Google once it's live

A verified but empty profile won't do much. Fill in everything: hours (holidays included), phone, website, a plain description of what you do, and real photos of your space, your team and your work.

Then ask your first happy customers for a review. Google lets you share a review link or a QR code for exactly this. One hard rule: never offer a discount or a freebie in exchange for a review. Google treats that as fake content and prohibits it.

If you go to your customers (contractor, mobile groomer, house cleaner), Google says to remove your address if you don't serve customers there, and to list the cities or ZIP codes you cover instead. You can set up to 20 service areas.

Week 1: Apple Maps, Bing and directories

Google isn't the only map. Every iPhone user who searches in Apple Maps sees Apple's own place cards, and Apple Business is free to get started, with place card customization on Apple Maps included. Bing has its own listing tool too, Bing Places for Business.

Then there are the directories that matter for your trade: Yelp for restaurants and local services, industry sites for contractors, lawyers or wedding vendors, and your local chamber of commerce member list. Pick the ones your customers actually use.

The rule that changes everything: the same details everywhere

Name, address, phone number: write them exactly the same way on every site. "123 Main Street, Suite 4" here, "123 Main St #4" there, an old cell number somewhere else. These small mismatches blur your identity for search engines and confuse customers.

Five directories you keep current beat fifty half-finished listings you'll forget. Keep a simple spreadsheet of where you're listed and with which login, so you can update everything the day you move.

Can people find you in ChatGPT?

More and more people ask an AI assistant "who's a good dog groomer near me" instead of searching. There's no ChatGPT listing form to fill out. When ChatGPT searches the web, it works from what it finds about you online.

OpenAI documents that its OAI-SearchBot crawler is what surfaces websites in ChatGPT search answers, and that sites which block it won't be shown there. If you have a website, check that your robots.txt file doesn't block it.

Everything else is the same work you're already doing: a complete Google profile, consistent directory listings, real reviews, and a site that says clearly what you do, where, and for whom. Write your pages the way you'd answer a customer on the phone. That's the kind of sentence an assistant picks up.

Week 2: how to market a small business locally

Once people can find you online, get seen in real life. That's often where the first customers come from, long before Google sends anyone.

  • Introduce yourself to nearby businesses and to complementary trades. An electrician and a plumber who refer each other can share jobs for years.
  • Pick one social network where your customers already hang out and post consistently: the finished job, today's display, behind the scenes. Consistency beats polish.
  • Join local groups (your chamber of commerce, the neighborhood Facebook group, Nextdoor) and be useful before you sell anything.
  • Tell the people you know, clearly: what you do, who it's for, and how to reach you. It sounds obvious. Most new owners still skip it.

The U.S. Small Business Administration suggests writing a simple marketing plan, starting with a detailed picture of your audience and what gives you an edge over the competition. For a new business, one page is enough. Who you serve, where, and why they should pick you.

Weeks 3 and 4: how to get customers as a new business

Waiting for customers to show up means accepting slow months. Visibility takes time to pay off. Direct outreach can put meetings on your calendar in the first week, as long as you stick with it.

A method that works locally

  • Decide who you're after: what kind of business or customer, in which area. "Restaurants in downtown Columbus" is a target. "Everyone" isn't.
  • Build a list of about thirty businesses that fit, with a reliable way to reach each one.
  • Write a short message about their situation, not about you. Three or four sentences, one simple question at the end.
  • Follow up once or twice, a few days apart. Plenty of replies come on the follow-up, not the first email.

What the law says about cold email in the U.S.

The CAN-SPAM Act covers commercial email, and the FTC is explicit that it makes no exception for business-to-business email. You don't need prior consent to send, but every message has to follow a few rules.

  • Accurate "From" and "Reply-To" details, and a subject line that matches the content.
  • A clear disclosure that the message is an ad, and your valid physical postal address.
  • An easy way to opt out, and you must honor opt-outs within 10 business days.

This isn't a formality. The FTC says each separate email in violation can bring penalties of up to $53,088. And if you hire someone to send for you, you're still responsible.

How to market a new business without money

If you're starting solo with no contacts and no budget, don't spread yourself thin. Do three things, in this order: a verified Google profile, one page that explains your offer, then twenty well-targeted outreach messages a week.

You don't need to be everywhere. You need to be findable by the people you contact, and consistent. An hour a day of outreach for a month usually beats one big push you never repeat.

The best way to market a new business is rarely the flashiest. Paid ads can wait until you know which customers say yes and why. Until then, every free channel in this guide is a way to find that out.

FAQ

How long does it take to show up on Google Maps?

Creating the profile takes minutes, but Google says verification reviews take up to 5 business days, and most postcard codes arrive within 14 days. Start the process the day you open.

How do I get my business on Google Maps for free?

Go to business.google.com/add, search for your business, claim it if it already exists or create it if not, then complete verification. Google says you can add or claim your business at no charge.

How do I get my small business noticed with no marketing budget?

Be findable first (verified Google profile, consistent directory listings, one clear page about your offer), then reach out directly: a targeted list of about thirty businesses, a short personal message, one or two follow-ups. Consistency matters more than volume.

Is it legal to send cold emails to businesses in the U.S.?

Yes, if you follow CAN-SPAM, which the FTC says applies to business-to-business email too. Use accurate sender details and subject lines, identify the message as an ad, include your physical postal address, offer an easy opt-out and honor it within 10 business days.

Can I offer a discount for Google reviews?

No. Google prohibits offering incentives, like free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for reviews. Just ask happy customers, using a review link or QR code.

How do I promote a business for free?

Complete your Google Business Profile and Apple Maps place card, list yourself on a few directories your customers use, join local groups, partner with complementary businesses, and contact prospects directly. All of these cost time, not money.
